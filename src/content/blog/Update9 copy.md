# Stella's Guide To Experimentation

*This is a guide on how to get good at baking through the reliance of experimentation, required learning and practice instead of mindless recipe-following. This is not intended to teach any specific skill required to bake any specific good, but to guide you in treating baking as an art form rather than a simple past-time.*

There is no universally agreed upon amount of categories of baked goods, but most if not all can be classified as one of these:
- **Yeast Breads** – Doughs leavened with yeast, resulting in airy, chewy baked goods like loaves, rolls, bagels, and pizza.
- **Quick Breads** – Baked goods leavened with baking powder or baking soda instead of yeast, such as muffins, banana bread, and scones.
- **Cakes & Bars** – Sweet baked goods made from batter, including layer cakes, loaf cakes, cupcakes, brownies, and blondies.
- **Pastries** – Dough-based baked goods with flaky, laminated, or enriched doughs, such as croissants, Danish pastries, éclairs, and puff pastry.
- **Cookies** – Small, individually portioned baked treats made from a relatively firm dough or batter.
- **Pies & Tarts** – Pastry-crust desserts or savory dishes filled with fruit, custard, meat, or other fillings.
- **Confections** – Sweets that rely more on sugar work than baking, such as fudge, brittle, caramel, truffles or brigadeiro.
- **Savory Bakes** – Non-sweet baked dishes like quiche, calzones, sausage rolls, and savory pastries.

You don't need to nerd out and learn what constitutes a certain baked good into which category, but knowing which are which can help you find more recipes in the direction you are looking for, as well as providing a sense of orientation if you wish to specialise.

### How To Progress

If you are only starting out, it's a good idea to start with the basics. Making different types of cookies will help introduce you to the baking-world and teach you what each ingredient does. Butter, sugar, baking powder, eggs, flour, and so on. Try following a recipe and then recreating it on your own when you feel ready. For most people, it is recommended to use a scale to measure the amount of each ingredient carefully. However when baking familiar recipes further down the road, especially simpler ones such as basic cookies, you may choose to omit it.

When you’ve got cookies down, try expanding your knowledge by baking with unknown ingredients and techniques. You can make simple confections, muffins, or a yeast bread, for example. Learning which combinations of ingredients and methods produce different results this way helps you build intuition for how recipes are constructed. Pizza dough and cookies both contain flour, but they behave very differently because of what else is included and how they are handled. Understanding this lets you start adjusting things like texture, structure, or even turning something traditionally sweet into something savory.

Not locking yourself into one category is especially important when starting out. At the same time, trying a variety of recipes across different types of baked goods gives you exposure to different techniques and outcomes. Looking around bakeries and patisseries can expand your horizons and spark ideas. Searching online, using books, and asking around can do the same. Limiting yourself to a specific country’s baking traditions and then comparing it with another can also help broaden what you choose to experiment with.

Depending on the recipe, the mix/dough may be kneaded, mixed, folded together, or a combination of those. Knowing how treating certain ingredients affects the end result (such as separating the yolk and egg whites when making muffins) is also crucial to replicating things correctly even without a recipe.

When you've gathered some experience working with a couple different techniques and ingredients, it is highly recommended that you make up your own recipe. Maybe you combine an element of cakes with cookies and create Cake Batter Chocolate Chip Cookies. You can get insanely creative. In fact, I did not know that those were a thing before I googled it 10 seconds ago. Anything is possible as long as you grasp the fundamentals of everything you're standing on. You can mix and match categories, or perhaps just slightly modify sensitive recipes such as macarons to find something that is fully your own. Macarons are due to their difficulty not the best example, but you get what I'm saying.

To turn experimentation into a repeatable skill, start with a base recipe and change only one variable at a time. This could be an ingredient, a ratio, the mixing method, oven temperature, or baking time. Keep everything else identical so you can clearly see what the change actually does. Record or remember the outcome and compare it directly to the original. Repeating the same change more than once helps confirm whether the result is consistent or accidental.

To truly stop relying on recipes, you need to understand Bakers' Percentages (or bakers' math). In this system, the total weight of the flour is always 100%, and every other ingredient is calculated as a percentage relative to that flour weight. For example, if a bread dough uses 500 grams of flour and 350 grams of water, the hydration is 70% (350÷500×100). This mathematical framework is a necessary tool for advanced experimentation: it allows you to instantly scale a recipe up or down to make any yield, troubleshoot an odd dough at a glance, and easily swap ingredients while maintaining structural balance. Once you memorize standard ratios, such as a classic 3:2:1 pie dough (100% flour, 66% fat, 33% liquid), you can create your own custom recipes from scratch without a safety net.

Learning to interpret results is just as important as making the change itself. More spread in cookies often points to changes in fat, sugar, or temperature. A denser texture can come from gluten development or reduced leavening. Drier results usually indicate time or ratio imbalance. Stronger browning is often linked to sugar content and heat interaction. Over time, these patterns turn into intuition.

Once this loop becomes familiar it stops being trial and error. At that point, recipes become reference points rather than instructions, and baking becomes a process of controlled creation rather than repetition.
